In Poland, can you record a call without asking everyone else first?

If you are an active participant, the answer is generally **yes under Polish criminal interception law**. You are recording information addressed to you, rather than secretly obtaining a conversation between other people.

That does not mean “consent never matters.” Recording, storing, transcribing, sharing, publishing, or using the call at work are different legal acts. GDPR, employment rules, confidentiality duties, personal rights, and the law of another participant's location may still require notice, a lawful basis, or permission.

The same distinction appears around the world. Some jurisdictions have a clear participant exception or one-party-consent rule. Others require everyone to agree. Several sit in between.

This guide gives a practical starting point as of September 24, 2026. It is not legal advice, and the country list is not exhaustive.

## The correct phrase is “one-party consent”

“No consent required” is convenient shorthand, but it hides the legal mechanism.

In a one-party jurisdiction, **one person who is actually part of the conversation can usually consent to the recording**. If you are participating and operating the recorder, that consenting person is you.

The rule does not normally allow you to:

- leave a recorder in a room and capture other people's private conversation;
- activate a bot in a meeting you do not attend;
- bypass confidentiality, professional-secrecy, or trade-secret duties;
- publish private audio without considering separate privacy and personality rights; or
- ignore stricter law when another participant joins from a different country or US state.

Participation is the boundary. Merely owning the meeting room, calendar event, company laptop, or conferencing account does not necessarily make you a participant.

## Poland: an active participant can generally record

Article 267 of the Polish Criminal Code addresses obtaining information to which a person is not entitled, including through a listening or recording device.

Poland's Commissioner for Human Rights has explained that this provision does not ordinarily apply when [one participant records their own conversation](https://bip.brpo.gov.pl/sites/default/files/Informacja_kwartalna_o_pracy_RPO_pazdziernik_%E2%80%93_grudzien_2006_r.pdf): information spoken to that participant was intended for them.

The Polish Supreme Court drew the same distinction in [case II CSK 478/15](https://www.sn.pl/sites/orzecznictwo/orzeczenia3/ii%20csk%20478-15-1.pdf). A participant recording information they received lawfully is different from a third party illegally intercepting a conversation.

The Court also warned that the circumstances still matter. A recording may raise serious concerns if it exploits another person's difficult situation, mental state, or vulnerability, or otherwise seriously violates principles of social coexistence.

So the practical Polish rule is:

| Situation | Starting point |
|---|---|
| You actively participate in the call and record it for your own record | Generally outside the criminal interception offence |
| You place a device or bot in a conversation you do not attend | Potential unlawful interception under Article 267 |
| You upload a work call for transcription or analysis | Separate GDPR, security, confidentiality, and processor questions arise |
| You share or publish the recording | Separate personal-rights, privacy, defamation, confidentiality, and data-protection analysis |
| An employer systematically records workers | Employment monitoring and GDPR duties apply; participant status alone is not enough |

Poland is therefore a good example of a **participant-recording country**, not a country where recordings are legally consequence-free.

## Countries with a participant or one-party starting point

The following countries have relatively clear statutes or high-court authority indicating that a participant is generally outside the criminal offence aimed at third-party interception. The final column is essential: it identifies why “I was on the call” may still be insufficient for a business workflow.

| Country | Why a participant may record | Main qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Poland** | Article 267 targets obtaining information not intended for the recorder; the Supreme Court distinguishes participant recording from illegal interception | Personal rights, GDPR, employment rules, confidentiality, and harmful use remain separate |
| **Canada** | [Criminal Code §184(2)(a)](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/section-184.html) permits interception with express or implied consent from the originator or intended recipient; §183.1 says one sender or recipient is sufficient | Provincial privacy, employment, consumer, health, and professional rules can add duties |
| **Denmark** | [Criminal Code §263](https://www.retsinformation.dk/eli/lta/2025/1294) criminalises secretly recording conversations “between others” or closed meetings the recorder does not attend | Authorities, courts, workplaces, data protection, and later disclosure can be governed separately |
| **Sweden** | [Criminal Code Chapter 4 §9a](https://www.government.se/contentassets/7a2dcae0787e465e9a2431554b5eab03/the-swedish-criminal-code.pdf) covers conversations between others and meetings in which the recorder is not participating | GDPR, employment duties, confidentiality, and dissemination still require analysis |
| **Norway** | [Criminal Code §205](https://lovdata.no/dokument/NL/lov/2005-05-20-28/) targets secret recording of communication between others or a closed meeting the recorder does not attend | Workplace control measures and use or publication of the result can trigger other rules |
| **Netherlands** | [Criminal Code Articles 139a–139b](https://wetten.overheid.nl/BWBR0001854/) distinguish a participant from a non-participant secretly recording a conversation | Organisations still need a GDPR basis, transparency, minimisation, retention, and security |
| **Finland** | [Criminal Code Chapter 24 §5](https://www.finlex.fi/api/media/statute-foreign-language-translation/689270/mainPdf/main.pdf) protects speech not intended for the recorder's knowledge; a participant is an intended recipient | Disseminating private information is separately restricted, and workplace processing has its own rules |
| **Spain** | The Constitutional Court has held that [a participant recording a conversation does not, by that fact alone, violate communications secrecy](https://boe.es/buscar/doc.php?id=BOE-T-1984-27955) | Core personal or family intimacy, data protection, employment surveillance, and disclosure can change the result |
| **Italy** | The Court of Cassation treats participant recording as [memorialising information lawfully received by one interlocutor](https://www.cortedicassazione.it/resources/cms/documents/Relazione_55_05.pdf), rather than third-party interception | Recording in specially protected places, professional secrecy, workplace use, and dissemination require separate review |

This table is deliberately narrower than many global “recording laws” lists. It includes jurisdictions where an official statute or court source supports the participant distinction. It does not imply identical rules, identical evidentiary treatment, or permission to use a recording for any purpose.

## New Zealand: possible does not mean automatically fair

New Zealand illustrates why a criminal-law label can be misleading.

The Office of the Privacy Commissioner says the Privacy Act will often not apply to a genuinely personal or domestic recording. But outside that setting, covert recording [may breach the Privacy Act](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/knowledge-base/view/31/), depending on who records, why, and how intrusive the collection is.

For workplace meetings, the Commissioner says recording with consent is generally best and that covert recording carries a strong presumption of unfairness unless compelling circumstances justify it. Participants may also have access rights to a recording made on behalf of an organisation. See the Commissioner's [meeting-recording guidance](https://www.privacy.org.nz/resources-and-learning/knowledge-base/view/324/).

The useful classification is therefore not “New Zealand requires everyone” or “New Zealand requires nobody.” A participant may be able to make a personal recording, while an employer using the same device for systematic monitoring faces much stricter obligations.

## US states where one participant is generally enough

US federal law supplies a one-party baseline. [18 U.S.C. §2511(2)(d)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A18+section%3A2511%29) generally permits interception when the recorder is a party or one party has given prior consent, unless the purpose is criminal or tortious.

State law can be stricter. Based on the [Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press state guide](https://www.rcfp.org/reporters-recording-guide/), the following 34 states generally use a one-party starting point for both in-person and telephone communications:

**Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Minnesota, Mississippi, Nebraska, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia, Wisconsin, and Wyoming.**

**Washington, DC** also generally follows one-party consent.

The recorder must still be a participant or have consent from a participant. Expectation of privacy, hidden-camera law, an unlawful purpose, workplace policy, biometric processing, and disclosure can create separate liability.

## Seven states with participant-friendly but mixed rules

These states should not be placed in a simple green “one-party” bucket:

| State | Participant-friendly part | Stricter part |
|---|---|---|
| Connecticut | In-person conversations generally use one-party consent | Telephone calls have stricter all-party and civil requirements |
| Nevada | In-person conversations generally use one-party consent | Telephone calls are treated as all-party |
| Missouri | Telephone calls generally use one-party consent | RCFP describes in-person conversations as requiring all parties |
| Oregon | Telephone calls generally use one-party consent | In-person conversations generally require notice or consent from all, subject to exceptions |
| Hawaii | Generally one-party | All-party consent may apply in a particularly private place |
| Maine | Generally one-party | All-party consent may apply in a particularly private place |
| Michigan | A participant may generally record their own conversation | A third-party eavesdropper is subject to the stricter all-party rule |

For the remaining primarily all-party jurisdictions and detailed qualifications, use Summit's [complete US state-by-state meeting recording guide](/blog/us-meeting-recording-consent-laws).

## GDPR does not turn Europe into one consent zone

GDPR does not create a single European rule about whether pressing Record is a criminal offence. National interception, privacy, and communications laws answer that first question.

GDPR governs a different layer: processing personal data in the recording.

For a company, freelancer, employer, or professional practice, that commonly means:

- identifying a lawful basis under [GDPR Article 6](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj);
- providing transparent information about purpose, recipients, retention, and rights;
- collecting only what is necessary;
- protecting audio, transcripts, summaries, and speaker labels;
- signing appropriate processor terms with transcription and AI vendors;
- handling sensitive or special-category data with an additional Article 9 condition where required; and
- deleting the material when the justified purpose ends.

Consent is not the only possible GDPR lawful basis. But replacing consent with “legitimate interests” is not automatic. The organisation must show necessity, balance its interests against participants' rights, and consider a less intrusive method such as manual notes or local transcription.

The personal or household exemption can remove genuinely private activity from GDPR's scope. It does not normally cover a business recording customers or an employee building a searchable archive for work.

## Recording and using are separate decisions

Even where participant recording is lawful, each later step needs its own answer.

### Keeping it privately

A short-lived recording used to correct personal notes creates a different risk from a permanent searchable archive.

### Transcribing it with an AI service

Uploading audio can disclose it to a transcription provider, foundation-model vendor, analytics service, and subprocessors. A lawful local recording does not automatically authorise those transfers.

### Sharing it internally

A manager may be entitled to attend a call but not entitled to distribute every statement to an entire department. Apply purpose limitation and least-privilege access.

### Publishing it

Publication can trigger personal rights, confidentiality, defamation, data protection, copyright, trade-secret, and professional-secrecy rules even when capture itself was lawful.

### Using it as evidence

Courts distinguish how a recording was obtained from whether it is admissible, authentic, relevant, or outweighed by privacy and fairness concerns. Do not edit or destroy potentially relevant material after a dispute is foreseeable; obtain legal advice.

## Cross-border calls: use the stricter rule

Suppose a participant in Warsaw records colleagues in Berlin, Zurich, and California. Polish participant-recording law does not guarantee that Polish law is the only law a court or regulator will apply.

Relevant factors can include:

- where each participant was located;
- where the recorder and recording system were located;
- which organisation determined the purpose;
- where harm occurred;
- the employment or customer contract; and
- the forum hearing the dispute.

The safe operational rule for international or interstate meetings is to notify everyone and obtain affirmative agreement before capture. A one-party rule is most useful for understanding the legal floor or exceptional personal situations—not for designing a silent multinational recording program.

## A practical decision tree

Before recording, ask:

1. **Am I actually participating?** If not, do not rely on one-party consent.
2. **Where is everyone located?** Use the strictest plausible rule for a cross-border call.
3. **Is this personal or organisational?** Business and employment use brings data-protection and monitoring duties.
4. **Why is a recording necessary?** Prefer a less intrusive method when it achieves the same purpose.
5. **Where will the data go?** Include transcription, summarisation, integrations, backups, and model-training terms.
6. **Who will receive it?** Capture permission is not publication permission.
7. **When will it be deleted?** Set retention before pressing Record.

## The practical takeaway

Poland does generally allow an active participant to record their own conversation without first obtaining every other participant's permission under criminal interception law. Canada and several European countries have comparable participant exceptions, while most US jurisdictions use a one-party baseline.

But “I may press Record” answers only the first question.

For a defensible workflow, separate capture from storage, AI processing, workplace monitoring, sharing, publication, and evidence. For cross-border calls, give notice and obtain everyone's agreement even when the recorder's home jurisdiction would permit silent participant recording.
