Can you record a meeting in the United States if you are part of it?

Under federal law, generally yes: the federal Wiretap Act has a one-party-consent exception. State law can impose a stricter rule, however, and modern meetings routinely include people in several states at once.

That is why a simple “one-party state” map is not enough. Some states distinguish in-person conversations from phone or electronic communications. Some protect only confidential or private conversations. Some treat a participant differently from an outside eavesdropper. Courts can also apply the stricter state's law to an interstate call.

This guide gives a practical starting point for all 50 states and Washington, DC. It is not legal advice. Recording laws can carry criminal penalties and civil liability, and the answer depends on the facts. Verify the current statute and obtain advice for sensitive, covert, interstate, employment, healthcare, legal, or investigative recording.

## The federal baseline

[18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A18+section%3A2511%29) generally allows a person to intercept a communication when that person is a party to it or one party has given prior consent.

The exception does not apply when the interception is for a criminal or tortious purpose. Federal law also does not give a meeting participant permission to ignore a stricter state law.

In ordinary language:

- **One-party consent** means one participant can usually consent to the recording. If you are participating, that person can be you.
- **All-party consent** means every participant covered by the law must consent. “Two-party consent” is a common label but becomes inaccurate in a meeting with three or more people.
- **No-party consent** is not a lawful shortcut. An outsider generally cannot secretly intercept a private conversation merely because it occurs in a one-party state.

## The states that do not fit a red-or-green map

The RCFP introduction identifies about eleven states as primarily all-party jurisdictions: California, Delaware, Florida, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan for at least third-party recording, Montana, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, and Washington.

It separately identifies four mixed states:

- **Missouri and Oregon:** stricter for in-person conversations, one-party for phone calls;
- **Connecticut and Nevada:** stricter for phone calls, one-party for in-person conversations.

Hawaii and Maine add another fact-sensitive category: one-party is the general rule, but conversations in particularly private places can require everyone's consent.

Those distinctions explain why lists on the internet disagree. Some count only the primarily all-party states. Others add mixed states. Some treat Michigan as all-party without explaining its participant exception. Others label an entire state based on telephone law while ignoring in-person law.

The correct answer begins with the communication, not the colour on a map.

## “Private” and “confidential” are factual questions

Many recording statutes protect conversations only when participants have a reasonable expectation of privacy or confidentiality.

Courts may consider:

- whether the conversation occurred in a home, office, public street, conference room, or open meeting;
- whether bystanders could hear it;
- whether the participants lowered their voices or closed a door;
- whether recording devices were visible;
- whether a platform gave notice;
- the sensitivity of the subject; and
- what the participants said about confidentiality.

A public location does not automatically make every conversation public. A private office does not automatically make every word confidential. The analysis is specific to context and state law.

For routine business meetings, relying on the absence of an expectation of privacy is usually worse than giving clear notice and obtaining agreement.

## Interstate and remote meetings

A Zoom, Meet, Teams, or phone call can connect a host in New York, a customer in California, an employee in Pennsylvania, and a vendor in Texas.

Which state's law controls can depend on where the recording occurred, where each participant was located, where the injury was felt, contractual choice-of-law terms, and the forum hearing the dispute. California's Supreme Court, for example, has applied California's stricter protection to calls with out-of-state businesses in circumstances involving California residents.

The most defensible operational rule is:

> When participants may be in different states, obtain affirmative consent from everyone before recording.

If that is not workable for a recurring process, have counsel define the permitted workflow and configure the product to match it. Do not ask hosts to perform a conflict-of-laws analysis live on every call.

## Does a platform banner count as consent?

It can be evidence of notice or implied consent, but the answer is not universal.

The RCFP guide notes that consent is often implied when a recording device is in plain view or people are warned and continue speaking. Some statutes expressly describe how an announcement can establish consent. Other laws, contracts, or organisational policies may require something more explicit.

A robust meeting flow should:

1. display notice before entry;
2. state the notice aloud when capture begins;
3. record an affirmative response when the risk is high;
4. repeat notice for late joiners;
5. provide a way to continue without recording; and
6. preserve the consent log with the artifact.

Silence is weakest when declining would mean missing a required work meeting, medical appointment, interview, or essential service.

## AI notes can still be a recording-law issue

Some products save video. Others save audio, a transcript, notes, or only a summary. Calling a feature “AI notes” does not decide whether interception or recording law applies.

The system may still acquire the contents of an oral or electronic communication. It may also create separate issues under biometric, employment, consumer-privacy, healthcare, education, or data-protection law.

Ask about each stage:

- Does the product capture system audio or microphone audio?
- Is raw audio buffered, transmitted, or retained?
- Is a verbatim transcript created?
- Are speaker embeddings or voiceprints generated?
- Does a human review any content?
- Who receives the summary?
- Is the content used for product improvement or model training?

Deleting the audio after transcription does not retroactively answer whether the initial capture was lawful.

## Employer policies do not solve every meeting

An employee handbook may notify workers that business systems are monitored or meetings may be recorded. That can be relevant, but it is not universal consent from customers, job candidates, contractors, family members, patients, or employees located in jurisdictions with stricter requirements.

It may also be too general for a new purpose such as emotion inference, voiceprints, performance scoring, or model training.

Employers should pair policy notice with meeting-level controls and a documented purpose. High-risk conversations—discipline, termination, accommodations, investigations, legal advice, and health matters—deserve a separate decision rather than an automatic bot.

## A practical recording checklist

Before capture:

- identify the participants' likely locations;
- determine whether the meeting includes sensitive or privileged material;
- choose the minimum artifact needed;
- confirm recipient and sharing settings;
- give clear notice and obtain the required agreement; and
- provide a non-recorded alternative.

During capture:

- keep the recording indicator visible;
- notify late joiners;
- stop when the audience or purpose changes; and
- avoid side conversations that fall outside the disclosed purpose.

After capture:

- restrict access to intended recipients;
- correct material transcript errors without hiding the original record;
- apply a defined retention period;
- preserve relevant material when a legal hold applies; and
- delete redundant audio, transcripts, summaries, and exports when authorised.

## The practical takeaway

Federal law supplies a one-party floor, not a nationwide permission slip. Eleven states are primarily all-party jurisdictions, six more have important mixed or private-place rules, and interstate meetings can bring the strictest rule into the room.

For organisations, the scalable rule is simple: give notice, obtain everyone's affirmative agreement, offer a non-recorded path, and stop capture when the meeting changes purpose. Then have counsel address the exceptions rather than asking individual hosts to improvise.

For individuals, “I was in the meeting” is not enough when another participant may be protected by a stricter state's law.
