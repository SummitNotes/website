An AI notetaker can give a board more detail than it has ever had: every question, hesitation, disagreement, side comment, and imperfectly phrased answer.

That does not make it a better corporate record.

Board minutes and meeting transcripts serve different purposes. Minutes document the proceedings, decisions, oversight, and process that matter. A transcript attempts to reproduce the conversation. Treating them as interchangeable can increase litigation, privilege, confidentiality, accuracy, and retention risk while making directors less willing to speak candidly.

The American Bar Association's Business Law Section put the point plainly in 2026: minutes should reflect that directors were informed and engaged, but they should not become transcripts. Its board-governance discussion strongly discouraged voice and video recording and described AI note-taking as problematic because of the sensitive information captured.

That is not a universal statutory ban on AI in the boardroom. It is a warning to design the record deliberately.

This article provides general corporate-governance information, not legal advice. Requirements differ by jurisdiction, entity, bylaws, sector, proceeding, and litigation posture.

## What minutes are supposed to do

Useful board minutes create evidence of a sound process. Depending on the meeting and applicable law, they commonly record:

- when and where the meeting occurred;
- who attended and whether a quorum was present;
- material presented to the directors;
- subjects considered;
- conflicts disclosed and recusals;
- advice received at an appropriate level;
- resolutions, votes, approvals, and dissents where relevant; and
- follow-up actions.

They should be accurate, objective, consistent, and concise. They need enough detail to show what the board considered and did without reproducing every sentence.

This is not merely stylistic. Corporate records can carry legal and evidentiary weight.

For Delaware corporations, [Section 220 of the Delaware General Corporation Law](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc07/index.html) defines corporate books and records to include board and committee minutes, records of board action, and materials provided to the board in connection with that action. Those materials may become central in a books-and-records demand and later litigation.

In the United Kingdom, [sections 248 and 249 of the Companies Act 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/46/pdfs/ukpga_20060046_en.pdf) require companies to record minutes of directors' meetings and retain them for at least ten years. Properly authenticated minutes are evidence of the proceedings, with statutory presumptions about the meeting and its actions.

Neither example says that “more words” makes a better minute.

## Why a transcript is a different record

A transcript records speech. Minutes record proceedings.

The distinction matters because board dialogue is rarely publication-ready. Directors test assumptions, ask deliberately provocative questions, change their minds, and explore risks before reaching a decision. Executives may answer provisionally and correct themselves after reviewing additional data.

A verbatim or near-verbatim transcript can preserve:

- preliminary views detached from the final decision;
- incomplete answers that were corrected later;
- emotional or ambiguous language;
- jokes, interruptions, and side conversations;
- detailed legal advice or litigation strategy;
- security, trade-secret, personnel, and transaction information; and
- transcription errors presented with artificial confidence.

That material may obscure rather than illuminate the board's process. It also creates a second account that can conflict with the approved minutes.

The [ABA's 2026 governance toolkit](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2026-june/process-is-your-friend-practical-toolkit-strong-board-governance/) recommends that minutes remain objective and avoid unnecessary detail about tense exchanges or distractions. It also stresses draft control: access should be limited and outdated versions should be deleted under the applicable policy.

An always-on AI workflow pushes in the opposite direction by creating audio, transcript, summary, action items, prompts, and revision history before the secretary has determined what the corporate record should be.

## Five risks boards should assess before pressing Record

### 1. A larger discovery surface

Recordings and transcripts can become relevant in litigation, investigations, regulatory inquiries, internal reviews, and books-and-records disputes. Deleting the audio immediately may not eliminate cached, backed-up, exported, or emailed copies.

The issue is not that detailed records are automatically harmful. It is that the company has created them without first deciding whether they are needed, accurate, appropriately retained, and aligned with the official minutes.

Once a preservation duty applies, ordinary deletion must stop for relevant material.

### 2. Privilege can become harder to manage

Board meetings often mix legal and business advice. A privileged label does not protect an entire transcript merely because counsel attended. Privilege depends on the purpose and content of the communication, the people present, confidentiality, and applicable law.

Third-party AI providers, broad workspace access, automatic sharing, and integrations add recipients. A transcript can also expose legal advice in far more detail than carefully drafted minutes would.

Local or contractually protected processing can reduce disclosure risk, but no vendor or architecture can guarantee privilege.

### 3. AI accuracy is uneven where precision matters most

Speaker attribution can fail in a room with overlapping voices. Names, financial figures, legal terms, product codes, and vote language are easy to mishear. A generated summary can turn a question into a position, uncertainty into agreement, or an instruction to investigate into a final decision.

The cleaner the prose, the easier it is to overlook the error.

Unreviewed AI output should never be treated as approved minutes, evidence of a vote, or the definitive account of a director's view.

### 4. More capture can reduce candour

Boards need directors to challenge management, surface uncertainty, and explore downside cases. Participants who expect a permanent, searchable transcript may speak for the record rather than think through the problem.

This is a governance cost, not just a privacy preference. A process that produces flawless documentation but weaker deliberation has failed the board.

### 5. The vendor may create an uncontrolled archive

Board content can include strategy, mergers, cybersecurity incidents, executive performance, litigation, forecasts, and material nonpublic information. A meeting product may copy that content into cloud transcription, model, analytics, integration, support, and backup systems.

Before any board use, map every recipient, region, retention period, administrator role, training term, export, deletion path, and incident obligation. “Enterprise plan” is not a data-flow diagram.

## A sensible default: no recording of formal board sessions

For many companies, the cleanest default is:

- do not record formal board or committee meetings;
- have a trained corporate secretary or counsel take contemporaneous notes;
- draft concise minutes using approved source materials;
- circulate drafts only to necessary reviewers;
- resolve corrections through a controlled process; and
- approve and retain the final minutes under the company's policy.

That position aligns with the [ABA's 2025 technology guidance for boards](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2025-april/10-tips-technology-in-boardroom/), which recommends deactivating audio and video recording for virtual board and committee meetings and directing proposed AI recording or summarisation back to the same concern.

The right default may differ where a law, regulator, accessibility requirement, proceeding, or specific evidentiary need calls for a recording. The decision should then be made deliberately with counsel—not inherited from a meeting platform's automatic settings.

## AI can still support the board without recording it

The choice is not “record every word” or “use no AI.” Lower-risk uses can support board work without generating a parallel transcript of deliberations.

Subject to company policy and appropriate review, AI may help:

- organise an agenda from non-sensitive planning material;
- summarise lengthy board books in a controlled environment;
- compare draft resolutions against a checklist;
- identify unanswered questions in written materials;
- format a secretary's human notes into the company's minute template; or
- check a draft for dates, names, defined terms, and internal consistency.

These workflows still require confidentiality, accuracy, retention, and privilege controls. They are easier to govern because the input is selected rather than captured continuously from live deliberation.

The human secretary remains responsible for the record. AI output is a draft aid, not the minute-taker of record.

## If a board chooses AI capture anyway

Some organisations will decide that the benefits justify controlled recording. The approval should answer more than “Is the vendor secure?”

### Before the meeting

1. **Confirm authority and consent.** Check applicable recording law, bylaws, board policy, contracts, and participant locations.
2. **Define the purpose.** State why a recording or transcript is necessary and why ordinary note-taking is insufficient.
3. **Select the authoritative record.** Usually this should remain the approved minutes, not the generated transcript.
4. **Map the data path.** Identify capture, transcription, model, storage, support, backup, integration, and administrator access.
5. **Set disposition rules.** Decide when audio, transcript, summaries, prompts, and drafts will be destroyed, subject to legal holds.

### During the meeting

1. Assign one recording owner.
2. Verify the participant and bot list.
3. Announce capture and its purpose.
4. Stop before privileged executive sessions or topics outside the approved scope.
5. Record formal votes and recusals through the secretary's controlled process rather than relying on model inference.

### After the meeting

1. Restrict the generated files to named reviewers.
2. Verify speakers, figures, resolutions, votes, and action items against authoritative materials.
3. Draft minutes according to the company's governance standard.
4. Approve the minutes through the normal board process.
5. Dispose of intermediary artifacts under policy unless preservation is required.

If the organisation cannot execute that workflow consistently, it should not record the meeting.

## The boardroom rule

Minutes should show that the board was informed, engaged, and deliberate. They should accurately document material process and action. They do not need to reproduce every word.

For formal board and committee meetings, default to human-controlled minutes and no recording. Use AI on selected written material where it adds value with less exposure. If recording is truly necessary, approve the purpose, system, reviewers, official record, and deletion rules before the meeting starts.

Good governance is not the maximum possible record. It is the right record, made through a disciplined process.
