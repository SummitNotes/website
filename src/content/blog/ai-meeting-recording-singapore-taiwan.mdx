Singapore and Taiwan do not share one rule for recording meetings with AI. Both regulate identifiable information, but their statutory structures, exceptions, notice requirements, and enforcement systems differ.

The safest operational lesson is simple: do not reduce either jurisdiction to “one-party” or “all-party” consent. An AI meeting workflow can involve several separate acts—capturing audio, transcribing speech, identifying speakers, generating a summary, sharing notes, retaining files, and sending data to an overseas provider. Each needs a purpose and a defensible treatment.

This checklist focuses on business meetings under Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) and Taiwan's Personal Data Protection Act (PIPA). It does not cover every communications, employment, sector, or criminal-law rule and is not legal advice.

## Start by mapping the meeting-data lifecycle

Before choosing a legal basis or drafting a notice, write down what the tool does:

1. Does it capture microphone audio, system audio, or both?
2. Is the recording retained, or discarded after transcription?
3. Are speakers labelled by name, account, or voiceprint?
4. Does a model create summaries, tasks, sentiment, or other inferences?
5. Is any content used to train or improve a service?
6. Which device, cloud region, vendor, and subprocessor receives the data?
7. Who can search, export, correct, or delete the result?

An answer such as “we only keep notes” is incomplete. A transcript or summary can still contain identifiable facts, opinions, performance information, health details, or confidential client material.

## Singapore: the PDPA obligations to check

Singapore's [PDPC overview of data-protection obligations](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/overview-of-pdpa/the-legislation/personal-data-protection-act/data-protection-obligations) describes a connected set of duties rather than a single recording-consent switch.

For an organisation using an AI note taker, the main obligations include:

- **Accountability:** appoint a data-protection officer and maintain suitable policies and practices.
- **Notification:** tell individuals the purposes for collecting, using, or disclosing their personal data.
- **Consent:** obtain consent where required, subject to the Act's exceptions and deemed-consent provisions.
- **Purpose limitation:** use data only for purposes a reasonable person would consider appropriate in the circumstances.
- **Accuracy:** make reasonable efforts to keep personal data complete and accurate where it is likely to be used to make a decision or disclosed.
- **Protection:** use reasonable security arrangements.
- **Retention limitation:** stop retaining data when the business or legal purpose has ended and retention is no longer necessary.
- **Transfer limitation:** protect personal data transferred outside Singapore to a standard comparable to the PDPA.
- **Access and correction:** support applicable individual requests.
- **Breach notification:** assess incidents and notify the PDPC and affected individuals where the statutory thresholds are met.

The PDPC's [Advisory Guidelines on Key Concepts](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/guidelines-and-consultation/2020/03/advisory-guidelines-on-key-concepts-in-the-personal-data-protection-act) are the starting point for applying these duties. Its [Selected Topics guidance](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/guidelines-and-consultation/2020/02/advisory-guidelines-on-the-personal-data-protection-act-for-selected-topics) also addresses audio and video recordings, employment, and cloud services.

### Common Singapore traps

**“It is a work call, so it is only business contact information.”** The PDPA excludes business contact information in defined circumstances, but the substance of a recorded conversation usually extends beyond a person's business coordinates.

**“An employee pressed Record, so the employee exemption applies.”** An individual acting as an employee may be excluded for acts done in the course of employment, but the organisation's own collection, use, and disclosure still need analysis.

**“The AI exception covers all AI notes.”** The PDPC's [AI advisory guidelines](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/guidelines-and-consultation/2024/02/advisory-guidelines-on-use-of-personal-data-in-ai-recommendation-and-decision-systems) focus on AI recommendation and decision systems. They are useful context, but they should not be treated as a blanket exemption or a perfect fit for every summarisation tool.

## Taiwan: when voice and meeting content become personal data

Taiwan's PIPA regulates the collection, processing, and use of personal data by government and non-government agencies. Its definition covers information that can directly or indirectly identify a natural person.

That matters for voice recordings. In an official interpretation, Taiwan's Ministry of Justice explained that [telephone-recording content can be personal data when it can directly or indirectly identify an individual](https://mojlaw.moj.gov.tw/LawContentExShow.aspx?etype=etype5&id=FE253692). A voice clip does not have to be a biometric template before privacy law becomes relevant.

For a private organisation, a meeting workflow should be tested against:

- the permitted grounds for collection or processing under Article 19;
- the notice items under Article 8, including the collecting entity, purpose, categories of data, period, area, recipients or methods of use, rights, and consequences of not providing data;
- the purpose limits and exceptions governing use under Article 20;
- security, retention, correction, deletion, and data-subject rights; and
- any applicable sector rules or government restrictions on international transfers.

The [Ministry of Justice's English PIPA text](https://mojlaw.moj.gov.tw/ENG/LawContentE.aspx?LSID=FL010627) is a useful reference, but the official Chinese text controls if translations differ.

Taiwan's PIPA analysis is not identical to the separate question of whether making a particular recording is lawful under civil, criminal, communications, workplace, or professional rules. Avoid turning privacy-law notice into a universal claim that every Taiwan meeting follows one consent formula.

## Singapore and Taiwan at a glance

| Issue | Singapore | Taiwan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core private-sector law | Personal Data Protection Act 2012 | Personal Data Protection Act |
| Is identifiable meeting audio personal data? | Generally yes when an individual can be identified | Yes when the content directly or indirectly identifies an individual |
| Notice | Notify purposes before or at collection, subject to the statutory framework | Article 8 lists detailed notice items, subject to exceptions |
| Consent or collection ground | Consent, deemed consent, or an applicable exception | A ground under Article 19 for non-government agencies |
| Use | Limited to notified and reasonable purposes, subject to exceptions | Generally within the specific collection purpose, subject to Article 20 exceptions |
| Overseas vendors | Transfer Limitation Obligation applies | Review PIPA, sector rules, contracts, and any competent-authority restrictions |
| Employee context | Employment-related provisions and exceptions require careful scoping | Employment and sector rules can affect the analysis |

## A cross-border meeting checklist

Use this before turning on an AI assistant for a Singapore–Taiwan call:

### 1. Give notice before capture

Identify the organiser, the fact that audio will be processed, the purpose of transcription and summarisation, what will be retained, who receives it, and how participants can raise a concern. Do not hide AI processing inside a generic meeting invitation.

### 2. Confirm the legal route in each jurisdiction

Document the relevant Singapore consent, deemed-consent, or exception analysis and the Taiwan Article 19 collection ground. Then assess any separate recording, employment, regulated-sector, or contractual constraints.

### 3. Offer a workable alternative

If someone objects, be ready to disable capture, take manual notes, or hold an unrecorded segment. An “alternative” that excludes the person from a necessary work meeting may not be meaningful.

### 4. Minimise what the tool collects

Disable video, emotion inference, voice identification, or full-audio retention unless the purpose genuinely requires it. Separate contact data from sensitive discussion and avoid recording privileged, health, disciplinary, or trade-secret material by default.

### 5. Review vendors and transfers

Record the hosting region, subprocessors, training terms, retention defaults, encryption, access controls, incident process, and deletion behaviour. A contractual promise is not enough if the actual product sends content elsewhere.

### 6. Set a short, enforceable retention period

Raw audio, transcript, generated summary, model cache, backup, and exported notes may have different lifecycles. Define each one and test deletion.

### 7. Human-review the notes

AI summaries can omit qualifications, assign an action to the wrong person, or turn a tentative idea into a decision. Review before sharing or using notes in an evaluation, dispute, customer commitment, or other consequential process.

## Why local processing can simplify the risk map

When recording, transcription, and summarisation remain on a user's device, fewer vendors may receive the raw conversation. That can reduce cross-border transfers, subprocessors, and third-party retention—important issues under both Singapore's transfer rules and Taiwan's data-governance framework.

Summit can run [meeting capture and AI locally on a Mac](/blog/zero-cloud-ai-meeting-notes), without adding a bot to the call. It also offers optional cloud and self-hosted model configurations, so the relevant assessment is the configuration an organisation enables, not the product name alone.

Local processing does not eliminate notification, purpose, legal-ground, security, retention, or recording-law duties. It is a data-flow choice that can make minimisation and control easier to demonstrate.

## The operating rule

For Singapore and Taiwan, treat every AI-recorded meeting as a data lifecycle, not a consent pop-up. State the purpose before capture, establish the legal route, minimise collection, review overseas recipients, provide an alternative, and keep only what the organisation needs.
