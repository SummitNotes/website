A team turns on end-to-end encryption for a sensitive meeting, then discovers that the familiar transcription and AI summary controls are unavailable. The immediate question is practical: can someone still produce useful notes without giving up the protection the team just chose?

There are two parts to the answer. Zoom and Microsoft Teams disable their native AI meeting assistance and transcription under end-to-end encryption. An authorised participant's device still has to turn encrypted meeting audio into sound, so local processing is conceptually possible at that endpoint. Whether a particular application can capture that audio, and whether the organisation permits it, are separate questions that need answers before the meeting.

The distinction matters because an encrypted conversation can produce a very ordinary document. Its transcript may eventually sit in a folder, appear in search results, or be pasted into an email. The call's encryption does not decide who can read those copies.

## What disappears when you enable E2EE

[Zoom's current E2EE documentation](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0065408) lists Zoom AI features, cloud recording and live transcription among the features disabled in this mode. Participants need a supported desktop or mobile app, or Zoom Rooms; the Zoom Web App and telephone dial-in are unsupported. Zoom describes meeting keys as being generated by participants' machines, with its servers unable to decrypt the relayed meeting data.

[Microsoft's current Teams documentation](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoftteams/teams-end-to-end-encryption) lists recording, live captions and transcription, and Microsoft 365 Copilot in meetings and events as unavailable with E2EE. Meeting organisers require Teams Premium; attendees do not. Microsoft also states that users subject to compliance recording cannot use E2EE because that recording requires access to the protected content.

Notice the difference in the recording language: Zoom lists **cloud recording**, while Teams lists **recording**. Those descriptions should stay intact when someone writes an internal policy or compares the products. Neither list, on its own, establishes that a particular external recording application is supported or authorised.

For meeting organisers, the practical consequence is worth addressing in the invitation. Someone who usually relies on a transcript for follow-up may need an agreed alternative. Someone who needs captions may require an accessibility arrangement before attending. Discovering missing capabilities after a confidential discussion begins puts unnecessary pressure on the person running it to change the security settings in a hurry.

## The audio must become usable at the participant's device

Encryption protects information within a defined boundary. Transport protection concerns the journey across networks; end-to-end encryption also controls which endpoints hold the means to recover protected content. A participant's computer must eventually decode audio for playback, because encrypted bytes alone cannot be heard through headphones.

That usable audio exists at the endpoint as part of ordinary participation. A local speech model could, in principle, process audio there and produce text without requiring the meeting service to transcribe it. This is an architectural observation, rather than a claim that every operating system, meeting client and capture application supports the combination. Audio permissions, capture interfaces, device configuration and administrative restrictions all affect an actual deployment.

It also helps explain why endpoint security deserves attention even when the network protection is strong. A person listening in the room, an unattended unlocked laptop, or software with access to local content occupies a different position from an intermediary carrying encrypted packets. Securing the connection addresses one set of risks; deciding what the receiving device may do with the content addresses another.

A useful review follows the whole sequence: the meeting client receives protected media, the device makes audio available for playback, an approved application may capture it, and a model may create a transcript or summary. At each step, ask where the content exists and who can access it. Describing the complete sequence prevents the phrase “encrypted meeting” from becoming a blanket assurance about everything that happens afterwards.

## Permission to attend does not settle permission to record

Consider a hypothetical product team discussing an unreleased acquisition with external advisers. Everyone is allowed to hear the discussion, but the organiser has agreed that no recording or transcript will be retained. A participant who runs a local transcription application would create exactly the record that the group had decided to avoid, even if no audio ever left that participant's laptop.

Another meeting might expressly allow one designated person to create a local transcript, retain it for a short review period and distribute only approved action items. The technical setup could look similar while the agreed handling of information is entirely different.

Before choosing software, establish which arrangement applies. The organiser and relevant administrators should know what will be captured, whether raw audio will remain, where AI processing will happen and who will receive the output. Participant notice and any required consent need to cover the real workflow. A vague announcement that “AI is helping with notes” leaves too much unexplained if the process creates a searchable, verbatim record.

An administrator's disabled recording control, an organisational ban on AI tools, or a meeting-specific no-recording instruction is a reason to resolve the policy question with the responsible people. Endpoint access must never become a pretext for circumventing those decisions. Where recording is prohibited, use an approved alternative, such as a jointly written decision log, or hold the meeting without a retained record.

The same care applies to manual notes. Although they can preserve much less information than a transcript, they may still contain confidential commitments or personal details. Agreeing on what should survive the meeting is usually more useful than beginning with a contest between note-taking tools.

## A transcript starts its own security history

Imagine an authorised transcript saved on a well-managed laptop after an E2EE meeting. The following morning, someone exports it into a shared project folder so colleagues can find the action items. Later, a paragraph is copied into a support ticket, where another team and an external supplier can read it.

The meeting connection has played no role in those later access decisions. Each destination brings its own permissions, retention behaviour and opportunities for further copying. Even a short summary can carry the most sensitive part of a conversation, such as a proposed redundancy, a negotiation limit or a customer's security incident.

For that reason, a review should include the intended final record. A team may need a full transcript to resolve a disputed technical requirement, or it may only need three decisions and their owners. Keeping the larger record because it was easy to generate creates a continuing protection obligation without necessarily improving the work.

The device holding the original also needs a realistic storage assessment. Check disk encryption, account access, screen locking, backups and the destination selected for exports. A folder that looks local in a file picker may be synchronised by another service. Deleting a document from the note-taking application may leave an exported copy elsewhere, so retention plans should name those copies instead of assuming that one delete action reaches all of them.

A sensible handover therefore includes both the document and its handling expectations: the approved audience, where the authoritative copy lives, and when any working audio or transcript should be removed. This can be brief enough to fit into the team's existing meeting procedure.

## Where local AI notes fit, with clear limits

Local processing can suit an approved workflow whose purpose is to avoid sending meeting content to an additional AI provider. Summit's [security architecture](/security/architecture/) documents local recording and transcription, with on-device summarisation available through Local Models. Its [model guide](/docs/general/local-models/) explains selecting Local in the AI Provider settings.

Those documented capabilities help explain the data flow; this article does not establish Summit capture compatibility with Zoom or Teams E2EE meetings. An organisation considering that combination should validate its supported devices, software versions and permissions in an authorised test meeting using non-sensitive material. The test should establish both whether capture works and whether the resulting content follows the approved route.

The configuration matters after capture too. Selecting a hosted AI provider sends relevant prompt content to that provider. Optional sync, export destinations and connected clients introduce further handling choices. Review these settings as part of the workflow instead of relying on the application's general description as local-first.

Summit's architecture also states that its local audio files and SQLite database are not separately encrypted by the application. Their protection depends on macOS controls and FileVault when enabled. That limitation belongs in the same discussion as the benefits of on-device processing: local storage makes the endpoint a central part of the security boundary.

## Agree on the record before choosing the tool

For a sensitive E2EE meeting, the most useful preparation is a short agreement about what the group needs to keep. Decide whether the record will be a transcript, a reviewed summary, a decision log or nothing at all, and assign someone to manage it. Then select an approved method that can meet those requirements on the devices people will actually use.

If local AI processing is permitted, test it beforehand, check the processing destination, and make the arrangement clear to participants. Review the output before sharing it, because an inaccurate attribution or an invented commitment can cause trouble even when every byte has stayed on an encrypted disk.

End-to-end encryption remains valuable when its boundaries are understood. It protects the meeting exchange while the organisation makes deliberate choices about the record that follows. Useful AI notes can fit within that design only when the technical setup, the participants' expectations and the handling of the resulting files all agree.
