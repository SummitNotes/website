A team can make a careful decision about an AI notetaker and still find itself using a service it would not approve today. The original review may have been sound: the right people read the terms, checked the settings, and agreed which meetings staff could record. Months later, the vendor adds a feature, changes a processing arrangement, or moves a control into a different subscription tier, and the approved workflow needs another look.

This is part of operating a SaaS service. The work continues after procurement because the product, contract, and organization's own requirements continue to change. A meeting archive makes that particularly consequential: a decision affects both tomorrow's conversations and the accumulated history already entrusted to the service.

The practical question is how to notice relevant changes early enough to choose a response. That requires a named owner, a record of what was approved, and an exit that can be executed if a new arrangement no longer fits policy.

## A small changelog entry can require a real decision

Zoom's [subprocessor page, effective October 2, 2026](https://www.zoom.com/en/trust/subprocessors/), offers a useful example. Its changelog records a new Microsoft processing activity and an updated trading name for MaestroQA. The current Microsoft entry includes Azure Speech capabilities and AI processing, with customer content and context conditional on AI features being enabled.

That is enough to prompt a targeted review. It is not enough to conclude that every capability in the current entry was introduced on October 2, or that every customer's meetings are subject to voice cloning. The changelog does not identify every changed phrase, and a provider list alone does not establish what happens in a particular account.

A reviewer should connect the published change to the services and settings the organization actually uses. Ask which feature invokes the processing, what information is involved, whether the feature is enabled, and whether the approved restrictions still hold. Save the answer with the relevant documentation and configuration evidence.

The same approach applies when an independent notetaker changes its speech provider or adds a feature that searches older meetings. The supplier's name may stay familiar while the processing relevant to a customer's approval changes. Equally, a public page may be clarified without a change to that customer's workflow. Treat the update as a reason to investigate and decide, rather than as a verdict in either direction.

## Preserve the reasons for the original approval

“Approved for business use” is difficult to maintain because it does not explain the boundary of the decision. A more useful record says which service and plan were reviewed, what content staff may submit, which features may be enabled, and which conditions must remain true. It also identifies the evidence behind those conclusions and when it was checked.

For example, a team might approve transcription for routine project meetings while excluding personnel matters, disabling external sharing, and requiring a particular retention period. Another might approve hosted summaries only for a designated group of users. These are illustrative choices; the point is to make the approved use specific enough that a later change can be compared with it.

Keep the applicable agreement, data-processing terms, relevant policy versions, provider information, and configuration record together. Preserve dated copies where permitted, alongside source links, because a live URL may later show different language. Record outstanding assumptions too. An unresolved question about backup deletion should remain visible instead of becoming an accidental assurance when a reviewer leaves the company.

Our [SOC 2 review guide](/blog/ai-notetaker-soc-2-security-review/) covers the initial assurance questions in more detail. Ongoing review adds a different task: identifying which parts of the earlier decision are affected by new evidence. Reopening only those parts can make the process manageable without pretending that the original approval lasts forever.

## Give monitoring an owner and a route to action

Assign a service owner who is accountable for receiving changes and getting decisions made. That person does not need to interpret every contract provision or technical change alone. They do need a clear route to the people responsible for security, privacy, records, procurement, and the business workflow when those judgments are required.

Use a shared operational address for notices where the vendor supports one, with coverage for absence and staff turnover. Subscribe to the relevant trust-center and subprocessor updates, review release notes and administrator announcements, and include renewal communications. A notification arriving in a former employee's inbox is little help to a team with a response deadline.

Choose a review cadence based on the sensitivity of the meetings and the consequences of an incompatible change. A highly restricted archive may justify more frequent checks and closer attention to planned releases. A limited deployment may need less frequent scheduled review, supplemented by prompt handling of material notices. Continuous responsibility does not mean a person must manually reread every policy every day.

The monitoring record can be small: when the change was received, the affected feature or term, its expected date, the reviewer, and the decision due date. Assign unresolved items to someone. An automated comparison can detect text changes, but a person still has to establish whether the change affects actual data handling or an approved requirement.

Review the workflow internally as well. If a team starts using the same approved tool for acquisition discussions instead of routine sales calls, the risk has changed even if the vendor's website has not.

## Review features and defaults alongside contractual terms

A subprocessor list covers only part of the relationship. A new search feature may make old records easier to discover; an integration may send summaries to another system; a changed default may alter who receives meeting notes. A plan change may remove a control the original approval relied on. These possibilities should be review triggers when they occur, not allegations about a particular vendor.

For each material change, establish the affected data, users, and time period. Determine whether existing records are involved, whether the feature is optional, and whether administrators can restrict it reliably. If the answer depends on a setting, check that setting in the deployed account and preserve evidence of the result. Documentation describes intended behavior; the configuration determines which options your team has selected.

Distinguish contract deadlines carefully. Section 5 of Zoom's linked [Global Data Processing Addendum](https://media.zoom.com/download/assets/zoom-global-dpa.pdf/dd327ebea27e11efb613d6ba63ed4cee), to the extent Zoom acts as processor, specifies advance notice of at least 30 business days for a new subprocessor and a 15-business-day objection period. It requires notice subscription and includes an emergency exception. Those provisions concern the specified engagements; they are not a universal review window for every policy, feature, or processing-description update.

Have the contract owner confirm which terms govern your account, the applicable deadline, and the available response. The operational review should finish early enough for that response to be useful. Waiting until renewal can be too late for a change scheduled to take effect during the subscription.

## Decide what would make you restrict use or leave

Write down decision triggers before an urgent notice arrives. A requirement that particular meeting content remain within approved processing arrangements, for example, should lead to a defined response when the vendor proposes an incompatible destination. Otherwise a firm procurement rule can quietly become an exception made under time pressure.

The response should match the actual impact. An optional feature outside the approved scope may remain disabled, with the restriction verified. A material ambiguity may require an answer before the affected workflow continues. An unavoidable change that conflicts with policy may require stopping new capture for certain meetings, arranging a replacement, or leaving the service.

Separate those actions in the decision record. Stopping future uploads does not resolve the treatment of existing records. Disabling a feature does not prove it can no longer process previously submitted content. Ask the specific questions needed to establish each effect and avoid assuming that one control solves the entire problem.

A useful decision states what may continue, what must change, who owns the action, and when it will be checked. If an authorized exception is available under company policy, document its scope and end date. If there is no acceptable exception, the migration needs enough time and resources to happen before the incompatible processing begins, where the available notice allows that.

Retain the reason when a change is accepted as well. “No action required” is much more useful when accompanied by evidence that the affected feature is outside the deployment or that the new terms remain within the approved requirements.

## Keep the cost of leaving visible

The ability to leave influences how freely a team can reject an incompatible change. If the archive is difficult to export, replacement workflows are untested, and links to meetings appear throughout the CRM, a short notice can turn a policy decision into an operational crisis.

Test exports periodically with representative, authorized records. Check whether the files preserve the information staff need, whether they can be read without the original subscription, and whether the team can locate the corresponding recordings. Estimate the effort needed to restore access restrictions and replace references in other systems. An export button says little about the labor involved in moving a working archive.

Our [Fathom-to-Fireflies migration review](/blog/fathom-fireflies-migration-privacy/) shows how specific that work becomes: credential handling, authorization, duplicate archives, retention, and validation all need decisions. Add overlapping subscriptions, staff training, and business interruptions to the estimate. Even a migration offered at no charge can consume substantial internal time.

Keep an approved fallback for future meetings, too. The team may be able to continue taking manual notes while an archive transfer is resolved, or use another already approved capture method. A temporary workflow should be usable enough that staff do not create unsanctioned accounts simply to keep doing their jobs.

## Local processing changes what you need to keep reviewing

A local workflow can reduce dependence on changes to hosted meeting-content processing. Summit supports transcription and summarization on the Mac, and its [local AI documentation](/docs/setup/local-ai) explains how to select that configuration. Keeping future content under local control can make some supplier changes less relevant to the meeting archive.

It still requires ongoing governance. Software updates, model licenses, device security, backups, and export destinations remain part of the review. Optional cloud providers introduce their own terms and processing arrangements; sync and connected tools need attention as well. Summit's [security architecture](/security/architecture/) describes those boundaries so the review can follow the chosen configuration.

The durable practice is to keep approval connected to evidence and retain a workable alternative. When a service changes, the team should be able to explain whether the change fits its policy, what it will do if it does not, and how long that response will take.
