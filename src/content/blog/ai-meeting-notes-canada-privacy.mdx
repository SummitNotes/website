There is no single Canadian privacy checklist that fits every AI-recorded meeting. The applicable rule can depend on the province, industry, employment relationship, purpose, and whether the activity is commercial.

At the federal level, the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA) remains central for many private-sector activities. Alberta, British Columbia, and Quebec have private-sector privacy laws that can apply instead of PIPEDA to in-province activity considered substantially similar. PIPEDA can still apply to interprovincial and international transactions and to federally regulated organisations, including their employee information.

Public-sector, health-information, labour, professional, and recording rules add further layers. This guide is a practical starting point, not legal advice.

## Are meeting audio and AI notes “personal information”?

Usually, yes, when the information is about an identifiable individual.

The data set may include more than a person's voice:

- the raw recording and video;
- transcript text and speaker labels;
- names, contact details, attendance, and timestamps;
- opinions expressed about an identifiable person;
- health, financial, disciplinary, or performance information;
- inferred tasks, sentiment, risks, or decisions; and
- metadata about device, account, location, or participation.

A summary does not become anonymous merely because it omits the recording. Context may still identify the speakers or people discussed. Voice also raises a separate biometric question when technical processing is used to uniquely identify someone; ordinary audio is personal information without necessarily being biometric data.

## Which Canadian privacy law applies?

Start with the organisation and activity, not the meeting platform.

### PIPEDA

The [federal PIPEDA text](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/P-8.6/) applies to personal information handled in the course of commercial activities, subject to its scope and exceptions. It also applies to employee information in federal works, undertakings, and businesses.

PIPEDA is organised around ten fair-information principles: accountability; identifying purposes; consent; limiting collection; limiting use, disclosure, and retention; accuracy; safeguards; openness; individual access; and challenging compliance. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner (OPC) provides a useful [summary of the ten principles](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/privacy-topics/privacy-laws-in-canada/the-personal-information-protection-and-electronic-documents-act-pipeda/p_principle/).

### Alberta and British Columbia

Alberta's and British Columbia's Personal Information Protection Acts regulate private-sector organisations within those provinces. Each has specific provisions for employee personal information. The details are not interchangeable, and notice can remain necessary even when consent is not the applicable requirement for a defined employee-information use.

### Quebec

Quebec's Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector has its own consent, governance, privacy-impact-assessment, technology, incident, and cross-border requirements. Organisations should not simply paste a PIPEDA policy onto a Quebec workflow.

### Other regimes

Provincial public-sector and health-information statutes can control meetings involving government bodies, hospitals, clinicians, schools, and other regulated organisations. Collective agreements, professional rules, contracts, and the Criminal Code may also matter. The common phrase “Canada is one-party consent” does not resolve an employer's privacy, transparency, or professional obligations.

## Consent must be meaningful—and sensitivity changes the analysis

Under PIPEDA, the appropriate form of consent depends in part on the sensitivity of the information and the individual's reasonable expectations. Organisations should explain their practices in a form people can understand, including the nature, purposes, and consequences of the collection, use, or disclosure.

In a [2026 joint investigation into OpenAI](https://www.priv.gc.ca/en/opc-actions-and-decisions/investigations/investigations-into-businesses/2026/pipeda-2026-002/), the federal, Alberta, and British Columbia privacy regulators said express consent is generally required for information that is sensitive or collected, used, or disclosed outside reasonable expectations. The Quebec regulator assessed the matter under Quebec's distinct framework. That decision concerned an AI service, not meeting note takers, but it is a useful current signal about consent, sensitivity, and reasonable expectations in generative-AI systems.

For meetings, ask:

- Would attendees reasonably expect audio to be retained, or only human notes?
- Are clients, job candidates, patients, children, or employees involved?
- Could the discussion reveal health, financial, legal, performance, or union information?
- Will content be used for training, monitoring, evaluation, or automated decisions?
- Can a participant decline without losing access to an essential service or work activity?

Do not bundle an optional meeting recording into consent for an unrelated service. Where consent is not the legal route, the organisation still needs an applicable statutory authority and transparent, proportionate processing.

## A Canadian AI meeting-notes checklist

### 1. Identify the organisation, activity, and jurisdiction

Record where the participants and organisation operate, whether the activity is commercial, whether the employer is federally regulated, and whether a provincial private-sector, health, or public-sector statute applies. Mixed meetings may engage more than one law.

### 2. Define each purpose before collection

“Productivity” is too vague. Separate purposes such as creating minutes, searching past discussions, assigning tasks, coaching staff, evaluating performance, or improving a model. A new secondary use deserves a new assessment.

### 3. Choose the consent or statutory route

Determine whether express consent, implied consent, an employee-information provision, or another authority applies. Document sensitivity and reasonable expectations. Give notice before recording starts and provide a realistic unrecorded option where needed.

### 4. Minimise recording and inference

Collect only what the stated purpose requires. Consider transcript-only processing with rapid audio deletion. Disable video, voice identification, sentiment scoring, or model training unless each has a justified purpose and legal basis.

### 5. Review the AI provider and data flow

Map hosting regions, subprocessors, support access, training terms, retention, backups, encryption, authentication, incident response, and deletion. Under PIPEDA, transferring data to a service provider for processing does not transfer the organisation's accountability.

### 6. Set retention by data type

Raw audio, transcript, summary, chat, model cache, and exported action items may need different periods. “Keep forever because storage is cheap” conflicts with limitation principles. Make deletion operational and include copies held by providers where required.

### 7. Protect access and exports

Use role-based access, device protection, encryption, strong authentication, audit records, and controls on public links. Meeting archives become especially sensitive because they concentrate searchable statements across many people and topics.

### 8. Verify accuracy before consequential use

AI can misidentify a speaker, lose a qualification, or invent an agreement. Require human review before notes affect hiring, performance, discipline, legal positions, healthcare, credit, or customer commitments. Provide a process to correct the authoritative record.

### 9. Support individual rights and complaints

Know how to locate data about a person across recordings, transcripts, and summaries. Publish a contact route, verify identity, apply the correct statutory exceptions, and preserve a record of decisions.

### 10. Prepare for incidents

Define what counts as unauthorised access, disclosure, or loss across devices and vendors. PIPEDA requires reporting breaches of security safeguards that pose a real risk of significant harm, notifying affected individuals, and keeping records of all breaches; provincial rules can differ.

## Special caution for employee meetings

Recording an external sales call and analysing an employee's weekly one-to-one are not the same use case. Employee notes can affect power, dignity, performance, discipline, and workplace trust.

Before using AI notes in the workplace:

- identify the employee-privacy law that actually applies;
- distinguish ordinary minutes from monitoring or evaluation;
- consult any collective agreement or workplace policy;
- restrict manager and administrator access;
- prohibit silent repurposing for performance scoring; and
- establish a correction and dispute process.

## How local processing changes the risk—not the law

Keeping meeting audio, transcription, and summarisation on the user's device can reduce disclosures to service providers, cross-border data flows, and the scale of a vendor breach. It can also make deletion and access control easier to align with the organisation's own systems.

Summit supports a [local-first, zero-cloud workflow](/blog/zero-cloud-ai-meeting-notes) on Apple silicon and does not need to add a bot to the call. Optional cloud and self-hosted providers are also available, so organisations should document and govern the configuration they select.

Local processing does not make PIPEDA or provincial law disappear. The employer or business is still collecting and using information, and a compromised endpoint can still expose it. The benefit is a smaller, more controllable data path—not automatic compliance.

## A defensible default

For most Canadian organisations, a defensible default is to announce AI capture before it begins, state a specific purpose, offer an alternative, collect the minimum, keep audio briefly or not at all, review notes before consequential use, and document the applicable federal or provincial framework.

That approach will not resolve every sector or province, but it avoids the most common mistake: treating a meeting recorder as a harmless feature instead of a personal-information system.
