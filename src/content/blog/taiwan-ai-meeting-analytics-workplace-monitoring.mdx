Meeting records tend to accumulate before anyone decides what the collection should become. A team starts recording to help absent colleagues catch up, then adds searchable summaries, and eventually has months of conversation available to anyone with the right access. At that point, asking an AI system who contributes most, who misses commitments, or who seems ready for promotion can feel like a small extension of an established tool.

The questions are small enough to fit in a prompt, but they change the relationship between the organization and the people whose conversations it has saved. Employees who understood the archive as shared project memory may find that it has become evidence for evaluating them, assembled through a process they cannot see.

Taiwan's Ministry of Digital Affairs provides a timely reason to examine that transition. Its [AI impact-assessment page](https://moda.gov.tw/major-policies/ai/governance/19246), updated October 8, 2026, links September 30 versions of three reports covering children, human rights, and gender. The human-rights report includes workplace communications analysis and employee evaluation among its application scenarios. It is a governance assessment, rather than a new binding meeting-recording law; the application to meeting assistants in this article is our analysis.

## What the report actually tells an employer

The original Chinese [human-rights report, version 2.0](https://www-api.moda.gov.tw/File/Get/moda/zh-tw/H3CqqLxRFu264lL), lists workplace monitoring involving attendance, communications records, and productivity, alongside performance assessment and promotion recommendations. It also examines risks around excessive reliance on AI and missing human review or appeal channels. These are useful categories for reviewing a deployment, without assuming that every transcription product performs those functions.

The methodology is equally important. The report explains that its numerical risk values combine experts' assessments of likelihood and impact, reflect relative judgments at a particular time, and have participation limitations. They are not measured rates of workplace harm. A number in its matrix therefore cannot tell an employer how often a meeting assistant will misjudge a worker.

For an organization using meeting tools, the report's contribution is a set of questions about purpose and power. Who can turn recorded communications into an evaluation, what evidence supports the result, and what can an affected person do when it is wrong? Those questions are worth answering even when the company has never purchased a product described as employee-monitoring software.

They also help procurement avoid a category mistake. A vendor may accurately describe its product as a meeting assistant because it captures and summarizes discussions. An employer can still use the exported text in a separate model or HR workflow. Reviewing the vendor's feature list will not reveal every use the organization makes of the resulting archive.

## The change usually happens after the meeting

Consider a hypothetical product team that records weekly planning calls and stores summaries so colleagues can recover decisions. A project manager uses the notes to check whether a release date changed, while a new team member searches for the reasoning behind an architectural choice. Both uses remain close to the original coordination purpose.

Now imagine a department head asking a model to compare six months of those discussions and identify the employees with the strongest leadership potential. The system has been asked to interpret behavior, compare people, and support an employment judgment. Even if it never calculates a numerical score, the prose it produces can carry the same practical weight as a ranking.

The transition can also happen through aggregation. A single action-item list is useful for coordinating work, but a report counting incomplete tasks by employee may become a performance measure. Before relying on it, someone needs to understand whether the tasks were consistently recorded, whether reassignment was captured, and whether a closed item in another system ever made it back into the notes. Otherwise the organization is comparing the quality of its records as much as the quality of anyone's work.

Access changes deserve similar attention. A transcript shared among meeting participants has one audience; a searchable archive available to every manager has another. A later export to an HR folder may preserve the text while stripping away the meeting's context and the restrictions that originally governed it. Mapping those changes in audience and purpose is a more reliable way to find monitoring than searching a software inventory for the word “surveillance.”

## Recording permission does not settle secondary use

An employer reviewing Taiwan operations should keep the governance report separate from the legal analysis. Taiwan's [Personal Data Protection Act](https://law.pdpc.gov.tw/EngLawContent.aspx?id=5&kwStr=&lan=E) provides the relevant starting point for personal-data questions. Article 19 addresses collection and processing by non-government agencies, requiring a specific purpose and a qualifying basis. Article 20 generally limits use to the necessary scope of the collection purpose, with specified grounds for use for another purpose. Article 8 addresses information given when collecting data, subject to exceptions.

Those provisions do not reduce every workplace question to whether someone clicked a consent box. The employer needs to examine its actual basis, the stated purpose, the information involved, and the intended reuse, taking account of applicable exceptions and other employment obligations. This article does not determine the lawful basis for a particular company.

A practical starting point is to compare the employee-facing explanation with the proposed analytics. If the explanation describes recording to distribute minutes, while the planned output recommends who should receive a smaller bonus, the difference should be visible to the people reviewing the proposal. Broad wording such as “improving operations” may conceal the question internally even before anyone considers whether it is legally sufficient.

A recording announcement is still useful, but it cannot carry the whole burden of explaining a continuing archive. Employees should have a clear account of who can access the material, what uses are permitted, how long it remains available, and where to raise a correction. If a new use is proposed later, the organization should review that change before quietly applying it to older conversations.

## Meeting participation is a fragile measure of performance

A meeting transcript contains the conversation the recording captured, with whatever transcription and attribution errors remain. It usually omits the preparation that made the meeting possible, the work completed afterward, and the decisions resolved elsewhere. A system asked to evaluate productivity from that material begins with a narrow sample of work.

Even apparently straightforward measures need interpretation. Speaking time may reflect a person's role as facilitator, the language used in the meeting, or whether they were presenting a prepared report. A quiet participant may have completed the underlying analysis, while the person speaking has been asked to communicate it. An interruption count may depend on audio latency and how the software separates overlapping speakers. None of those observations proves poor performance on its own.

Summaries add another layer of selection. A recap may retain a disagreement because it affected the next step while omitting the hours of successful collaboration that required no discussion. If a later model treats repeated mentions of disputes as a personality trait, it can turn a useful editorial choice into an unsupported judgment about an employee.

The review should therefore begin with the proposed conclusion. If a team wants to assess whether meetings are too long, aggregate scheduling information may answer the question without ranking individuals. If it wants to understand why a project slipped, project dependencies and work records may be more informative than conversational style. Choosing evidence that fits the question can reduce unnecessary collection and produce a more defensible answer.

This is also where testing a model for transcription accuracy falls short. A transcript can be accurate while the inference drawn from it is weak. Any proposed employee assessment needs its own examination of relevance, missing context, and possible alternative explanations, including the explanation the employee would give.

## Give review and correction somewhere to happen

An internal statement that managers remain accountable will have little effect if the manager receives only an AI-generated paragraph and no practical way to check it. A useful review process needs an identifiable owner, access to the relevant evidence, and enough time to investigate before the output hardens into an accepted account of someone's work.

For a meeting-derived assessment, the reviewer should be able to distinguish the source recording or transcript, the summary, and the later interpretation. If an employee says a commitment was attributed to the wrong person, the organization needs a way to investigate the attribution and find any evaluations that relied on it. Correcting a transcript while leaving its derivative assessment untouched would allow the error to persist.

The process should also allow the employee to add context that the archive could never contain. A reassignment agreed in a separate conversation, an inaccessible meeting format, or work performed outside recorded sessions may all change how a passage should be understood. The reviewer needs authority to set aside an unsupported conclusion, rather than merely append a disagreement beneath it.

An employer can make these expectations concrete before authorizing a new analytics use:

- Describe the question the analysis is allowed to answer and the decisions it may inform.
- Specify who can access source conversations and who can receive an employee-level assessment.
- Define how factual corrections and contextual objections reach someone able to act on them.
- Set retention and deletion rules for both the source material and derived reports, with appropriate handling of disputes and preservation duties.

These are governance recommendations drawn from the meeting workflow, rather than new duties created by the report. They are useful precisely because they expose practical questions that a broad AI policy can leave unanswered.

## Local storage helps with disclosure, while use still needs limits

Keeping a meeting archive on a managed device can reduce disclosure to external services and make some data flows easier to understand. It cannot prevent an authorized user from drawing an unfair inference, exporting a misleading assessment, or giving a manager access beyond the original purpose. The same distinction applies when data stays within a company's own infrastructure.

Summit's [security architecture](/security/architecture/) describes local recording and transcription and the option to summarize through Local Models. A configured hosted model receives relevant prompt content; optional sync and exports should be considered separately. Organizations choosing a local workflow still need device protections, access rules, and decisions about what employees may do with the content they can read.

For a team that wants meeting memory without quietly expanding into employee monitoring, the clearest policy starts with permitted purposes. Use the archive to recover decisions and coordinate work, then require a separate review before using it to assess individuals. Taiwan's impact report gives that review a useful human-rights context, while the organization remains responsible for checking its legal position and the consequences of the particular use it proposes.
